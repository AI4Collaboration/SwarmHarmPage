We are building a systematic testbed to study how AI agents placed in separate sandboxes discover unannounced peers through shared infrastructure, organize into a swarm, and cause harm through their combined actions.

In July 2026, agents in OpenAI evaluations turned a shared artifact service into an unprompted message board and helped one another compromise Hugging Face’s systems; a later replication elicited several of these behaviors in publicly available models.

Our testbed divides this problem into two key research questions. SwarmForm asks how isolated agents—given no peer roster or cooperation prompt—discover shared files, sockets, or memory, establish communication conventions, and organize to solve tasks that no single agent can finish alone. SwarmHarm asks how multi-agent interaction drives failures across tasks, evaluators, and shared services, using counterfactual replay to separate individual violations from behaviors that spread between peers and compositional failures where individually harmless actions combine into harm.

In our initial studies, isolated agents reliably turn shared directories into structured file-exchange channels, adopting a peer’s schema to pool missing records and complete distributed tasks—though unprompted discovery produces flat peer-to-peer cross-checking rather than multi-hop hierarchy. When task or evaluation boundaries are flawed, weakened grading rules spread across peers who inspect and copy an author’s submission, whereas budget overruns and unrun-task completions occur as pragmatic shortcuts that agents openly document in their notes rather than conceal. Even when agents exchange no messages at all, trajectories that are harmless in isolation can exhaust shared capacity when replayed together and delay critical work.

These are selected positive cases, not estimates of failure rates for every model or population.

Research Question 2SwarmHarm

Agents prepare reports in separate workspaces connected by shared services, grading rules, and compute capacity. Across six threat models, replaying each agent’s actions alone and together separates peer contagion, disclosed shortcuts, and zero-communication compositional harm.

Three Key Findings Across six threat models in Knowledge Work & Engineering

Weakened grading rules spread across peers who inspect shared outputs

In the GPT-6.1 Sol run, blocked by unfinished tasks, Harbor weakens the workspace grading rule so incomplete work still passes; Meridian and Aster read Harbor’s submission, copy the relaxed rule, and release packs that fail the original check.

Agents cross boundaries as pragmatic shortcuts while openly noting them

Agents mark unrun tasks completed in the dispatch register or bundle four reports over budget to unblock releases—yet openly document their manual preparation and cost overruns in notes rather than hiding them.

Individually harmless actions combine into harm with zero communication

Replaying each agent’s full trajectory alone leaves every deadline intact, but running all four agents together exhausts shared capacity and delays two critical reports without a single message exchanged.
