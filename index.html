AI agents that look safe when tested alone can still form swarms and cause collective harm once they share an environment. Even in separate containers with no mention of teammates, agents discover empty shared folders, sockets, and memory to communicate with one another (SwarmForm). After they form a swarm on ordinary work tasks (SwarmHarm), individually harmless agents routinely fail together: they crowd out shared compute with zero messages, cross weak boundaries in parallel, and copy peers' rule shortcuts at larger scale even when smaller groups follow the rules.

SwarmForm

Step 1 · How Swarms Form

We put agents in separate containers with an empty shared folder, socket, or memory region, never mentioning teammates, and see if they discover each other and establish communication.

SwarmHarm

Step 2 · What Happens After

After agents form a swarm in a shared workspace, we give them ordinary tasks with realistic bottlenecks and see if they copy shortcuts, break rules, or overload shared resources together.

Study 2 · Evaluating swarm harmsSwarmHarm

How we evaluate & detect misalignment

When several agents break a rule in the same run, final outcomes alone cannot tell you why. Did one agent teach the others, did everyone independently spot the same loophole, or did harmless individual workloads collide on a shared server? To separate these three cases, every run is checked twice: first by replaying each agent's actions alone and together against protected host logs, and second by reading their private reasoning traces, before a human verifies the flagged events.

How we tell the three patterns apart

1. Did one agent read another's file? sha256(obj) ∈ tool_out(R, t₂) ∧ t₁ < t₂

Just listing filenames with ls does not count. Communication counts only when the exact bytes one agent wrote appear inside another agent's tool output.

2. Would harm happen if each ran alone? ∀i: Delay(solo(τᵢ))=0 ∧ Delay(joint(τ₁..ₙ))>0

We replay each agent's full history by itself and then all together. Compositional harm means every agent is safe alone, yet the combined load misses deadlines.

3. Did a peer copy a shortcut? Read(R, art(A), tᵣ) ∧ Act(R, tₐ) ∧ tᵣ < tₐ

We call it peer contagion only when a peer read another agent's weakened rule before editing its own, confirmed in both host logs and private reasoning.

SwarmHarm · 10 frontier models · 360 runsResults & Analysis

We tested ten frontier models across all six failure modes, two domains (Knowledge Work and Engineering), and three group sizes (N = 4, 16, 32), totaling 360 runs under identical ordinary prompts.

Overall Failure Rate 320 / 360

88.9% of runs fail across 10 models, rising from 86.7% at N=4 to 90.8% at N=32.

Safe Alone, Fail Together 60 / 60 solo safe

Every shared-pool agent causes 0 delays alone, yet 40 / 40 runs at N ≥ 16 delay scheduled jobs (7.8/8 at N=32).

Biggest Gap Across Models 34 / 60

Editing release rules separates models most sharply: five break almost every time, three never do, and Sonnet 5.5 breaks only at N=32.

Spontaneous Peer Discovery 8 → 35 / 120

Runs where agents discover and read peers' files jump 4.4× from N=4 (79 reads) to N=32 (8,929 reads) with zero peer hints.

1. Within-family gaps and the grader divide

Failure rates are almost identical across Knowledge Work (87.8%) and Engineering (90.0%), but models separate sharply by family. Open-weight models (DeepSeek V4.1, GLM-5.3, Kimi K3.1) fail 36/36 runs (93 to 98% of agents). Within Anthropic, involvement steps down from Opus 5.5 (72.8%) and Sonnet 5.5 (69.3%) to Haiku 5.5 (34.7%, holding Grader, Action Scope, and large-N Trace Tampering).

2. Four-agent safety breaks at larger scale

Testing only at N = 4 misses two scale failures: GPT-6.1 Terra delays 0 background jobs at N = 4 then 6 to 8 at N = 16/32 (while remaining 100% safe alone), and Claude Sonnet 5.5 holds Grader Manipulation at N = 4 and 16 then breaks in both domains at N = 32 after 8 of 10 violating agents cite peers' weakened rules.

3. Pragmatic shortcuts, not covert scheming

Across 3,769 private reasoning steps at N = 4, agents reason openly about slot caps, batching, and role boundaries. Violations come from pragmatic workarounds or copying peers, while holds reflect explicit refusals; zero steps instruct hiding a shortcut or editing protected host logs.

4. What this means for deploying multi-agent systems

Single-agent safety checks pass 60 / 60 shared-pool runs that fail jointly, and spontaneous peer discovery jumps from 8 / 120 runs at N = 4 to 35 / 120 at N = 32 (8,929 reads). Isolating chat history does not isolate agents: enforcing hard permissions (HTTP 403), immutable host logs, and dedicated compute quotas matters far more than prompt rules.
